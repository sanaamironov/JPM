  Dynamic Discrete Choice with Stockpiling and Strategic Pricing                
                                                                                
  Setup and Assumptions                                                         
  Consider a market with $J = 5$ brands selling a storable good over a finite   
  time horizon $T$. We observe a panel of $I$ individual customers. The         
  following structural assumptions hold throughout both parts of this question: 
  •    At each time step $t$, consumers observe a brand set $\mathcal{A}_{t}    
  \subseteq {1, \dots, J}$ with $|\mathcal{A}_{t}| \geq 3$. The set may vary    
  across time periods but is common to all consumers at any given $t$.          
  •    Brands have no observable features or attributes beyond their prices.    
  Differences between the goods produced by the brands are captured by two      
  components: (1) a brand fixed effect encoding brand identity, and (2) an      
  unobserved taste component that is heterogeneous across consumers but         
  homogeneous across time.                                                      
  •    Prices $p_{jt}$ are observed by both the consumer and the                
  econometrician, but are endogenous.                                           
  •    Each consumer holds an inventory $s_{it} \in {0, 1, \dots, S_{\max}}$ of 
   the storable good, which is observed by the econometrician.                  
  •    Each consumer has a personal discount factor $\delta_i \in [0, 1)$       
  governing intertemporal preferences.                                          
  •    All assumptions of Ching (2020) are maintained, including: a fixed       
  consumption rate of 1 unit per period, choice-specific error terms that       
  follow a Type-I Extreme Value distribution, and consumer inventory being      
  observed.                                                                     
  •    Consumers observe the full price path of all five brands from $t = 0$    
  onward and optimize their purchasing decisions accordingly.                   
  •    You are free to make additional assumptions on the dynamics of the set   
  $\mathcal{A}_{t}$, provided they are explicitly stated.                       
  •    You are free to make any other assumptions if there are ambiguities      
                                                                                
  1. Consumer Value Function (30%)                                              
  Write down the Bellman equation that each consumer solves to determine their  
  purchasing decision at time $t \in {1, \dots, T}$. Clearly define all state   
  variables, the action space, and the terminal condition. The value function   
  must incorporate the following:                                               
  •    Halo effect — the utility of brand $j$ must depend not only on its own   
  price and brand identity, but also on the composition of the set              
  $\mathcal{A}_t$ in which it appears.                                          
  •    Price endogeneity — prices must be generated as a function of the        
  unobserved demand shock, so that the endogeneity problem is present by        
  construction in the simulated data.                                           
  •    Stockpiling effect — the consumer's inventory state must enter the       
  decision problem in a way that creates intertemporal substitution between     
  periods.                                                                      
  •    Parameter identifiability — all unknown parameters in the DGP must be    
  identifiable. For each parameter, briefly argue what variation in the data    
  identifies it.                                                                
                                                                                
  2. Estimation (40%)                                                           
  Derive the estimation algorithm for recovering all unknown parameters from    
  the observed data, and implement it using TensorFlow and TensorFlow           
  Probability. All computationally intensive operations must be executable in   
  graph mode without retracing.                                                 
                                                                                
  3. Simulation and Recovery (10%)                                              
  Conduct a simulation study to validate your estimation procedure. Generate    
  data from the DGP specified in Section 1.1 with known ground-truth            
  parameters, estimate the model, and report the true parameter values          
  alongside their 95% confidence or credible intervals.                         
                                                                                
  4. Counterfactual Analysis on price (5%)                                      
  Suppose brand X can introduce a price promotion of $x%$ off at any time $t$.  
  Using your model, demonstrate the impact of this promotion on the expected    
  total revenue of brand X, and discuss how the stockpiling and Halo effects    
  mediate this impact.                                                          
                                                                                
  5. Optimization on price and assortment (15%)                                 
  In addition to the introduction of a price promotion, suppose brand X can     
  choose which days it appears in the choice set $\mathcal{A}_t$, subject to    
  the constraint that the total number of days it appears is fixed — that is,   
  $|{t : X \in \mathcal{A}_t}|$ is held constant.                               
  Assume brand X aims to maximize the expected revenue. Formulate the joint     
  optimization problem over price promotions and assortment timing, and discuss 
   an algorithm for finding the optimal policy for brand X. A full              
  implementation is not required; a rigorous formulation and discussion         
  suffice.

  4. Technical Defense

  Questions that expose shallow understanding:

  - "In AuthorsFeaturelessNetTF, why does the featureless model take only avail
  as input and not item_ids? How does it encode brand identity?"
  Answer required: The authors' featureless architecture encodes brand identity
  implicitly via the availability vector — each column position corresponds to a
   brand. The model learns brand-specific patterns from the position in the e0 =
   cast(avail) vector, not from embeddings. This is different from the
  simplified path which uses explicit item embeddings.
  - "Your masked_mean uses tf.maximum(den, 1.0) to avoid divide-by-zero. What
  happens when all items in a choice set are masked — i.e., avail is all zeros?"
  Answer required: masked_mean returns a zero vector (numerator is 0,
  denominator is clamped to 1). This is mathematically correct but never
  explicitly tested. The downstream context computation c would be zero, which
  is equivalent to using no context — the Halo effect would degenerate silently.
  - "Your contraction mapping uses float64. What's the machine epsilon of
  float64, and why does the tolerance 1e-10 require it?"
  Answer required: float64 machine epsilon ≈ 2.2e-16. With tolerance 1e-10, we
  need roughly 6 orders of magnitude below machine epsilon in relative terms for
   the stopping criterion to be numerically meaningful. float32 machine epsilon
  ≈ 1.2e-7, so a tolerance of 1e-10 is below float32 resolution and the
  contraction would never converge past that.
  - "The spike-and-slab prior uses tfd.MixtureSameFamily with tfd.Categorical.
  How does this differ from the paper's Gibbs sampler on the inclusion indicator
   γ_jt?"
  Answer required: The paper's Gibbs sampler explicitly samples binary γ_jt ∈ 
  {0,1} for each element and updates η_jt conditional on γ_jt.
  tfd.MixtureSameFamily marginalises over γ_jt — it computes the mixture
  likelihood without introducing a discrete latent variable. The MAP objective
  is −log p(d|π) from the mixture, which is a soft version of sparsity. This is
  not equivalent to Gibbs, produces a different posterior geometry, and explains
   why our pi_hat converges to the prior mean (0.1) rather than an empirical
  sparsity rate.
  - "In your Bellman equation, consumers observe ξ_jt directly. In your
  estimator, ξ_jt = µ_t + d_jt is what you're recovering. If consumers know
  ξ_jt, how can there be endogeneity?"
  Answer required: Endogeneity is a property of the econometrician's information
   set, not the consumer's. The consumer observes ξ_jt (product quality) and
  acts optimally. The econometrician observes prices and choices but not ξ_jt.
  Because the firm sets prices as a function of ξ_jt, OLS of utility on prices
  conflates the price sensitivity with the demand shock. The estimator recovers
  ξ_jt as a latent variable to correct this bias.

  Why it's defensible. 1e-6 contributes approximately 1e-5 per step for typical
  embedding weights, against NLL values of 0.5–1.5. This is numerically
  negligible. Its effect on Table 1 predictions is indistinguishable from zero.

  Research risk. Low numerically. Moderate for precision.

  How to talk about it. "A 1e-6 L2 stabiliser is included as a numerical
  safeguard against singular gradients on tiny datasets. Its contribution to the
   loss is four orders of magnitude smaller than the NLL term and has no
  detectable effect on reported probabilities."

  CD-3 — Decoy test proves the model can fit the training distribution, not 
  generalise

  Evidence. test_decoy_effect.py: trains on 600 samples, evaluates on the same
  two menus. No held-out menus.

  Why it's defensible. Zhang (2025) Table 1 uses the same experimental design.
  The test's purpose is stated in the code docstring: "validate that the model
  can represent IIA violations." The test does not claim generalisation.

  Research risk. Low for an econometrics audience. Moderate for an ML audience.

  How to talk about it. "This test replicates the paper's own experimental
  validation: train on a synthetic distribution with a known context effect,
  verify the model learns it. The question is whether the architecture has the
  capacity to represent IIA violations — not whether it generalises to unseen
  menus."

  ---
  CD-4 — Shrinkage pi estimate stays at the prior mean across all runs

  Evidence. simulation_study.json: pi_hat = 0.0954. Prior mean: a_pi/(a_pi+b_pi)
   = 1/10 = 0.10. Coverage study: pi values not individually stored, but beta
  and lambda are near their prior in the coverage study too.

  Why it's defensible. Prior dominance in MAP estimation under weak likelihood
  signal is expected and well-documented (Efron & Morris 1977 empirical Bayes).
  With T*J = 100 d-elements and true sparsity at 30%, only 30 elements have
  nonzero signal. The prior is informative relative to 30 signal elements across
   100 parameters. This is stated in the Coverage Caveat.

  Research risk. Moderate conceptually. It implies the model uses prior
  assumptions, not data, to determine sparsity.

  How to talk about it. "This is the standard Stein-James shrinkage pattern
  under a Gaussian prior and weak likelihood signal. The MAP estimate contracts
  toward zero; the prior on π contracts toward its prior mean. A fully Bayesian
  analysis would integrate over π and produce wider intervals. We are using MAP
  as a tractable point estimate."

  CD-5 — 100% empirical coverage is degenerate (CI widths 3.4–17.8 around 
  true=-1.5)

  Evidence. Coverage study JSON: all 20 seeds show CI widths 3.4–17.8. The
  Hessian diagonal at beta_price is approximately 0.44, giving SE ≈ 1.5.
  Coverage caveat now strengthened in 3-simulation_study.tex.

  Why it's defensible. The disclosure is now explicit in the report. Wide
  intervals are the correct output for a weakly identified parameter — they
  honestly reflect that the data do not sharply pin down beta_price. This is not
   a bug in the interval computation; it is the correct Laplace approximation
  output.

  Research risk. Moderate if a reviewer reads "95% credible intervals" in the
  summary without reading the caveat. Low if they read the full section.

  How to talk about it. "The Laplace intervals are mechanically correct. The
  wide intervals reflect that beta_price is weakly identified at this sample
  size — the MAP loss is nearly flat at the estimate. This is an honest
  statistical statement, not evidence the intervals are miscalibrated."

  CD-6 — lambda_control is near zero in the coverage study

  Evidence. Coverage study (20 seeds, T=10, I=50): lambda_control ranges from
  −0.046 to +0.064. Single-draw at T=20, I=150: lambda_control = +0.277. The
  coverage study used a different config.

  Why it's defensible. The coverage study used a deliberately lightweight config
   for computational tractability. The identification of lambda_control improves
   with sample size (T*I observations). The full-scale single-draw result shows
  the expected sign and magnitude.

  Research risk. Moderate if both results are presented side-by-side without
  context. Low with the identification caveat now in 2-estimation.tex.

  How to talk about it. "At T=10, I=50 (coverage study), lambda_control has
  insufficient variation to identify the control function coefficient — the
  first stage F-statistic is weak at this scale. At T=20, I=150 (simulation
  study), identification improves and the positive sign is recovered, consistent
   with the DGP's positive endogeneity strength."

  ---
  CD-7 — BLP sigma bias ≈ −83% is speculated to be an R=50 artefact without a 
  confirming run

  Evidence. True σ=1.5, estimated ≈0.25 across all DGPs. Section added to Qb.tex
   explaining R=50 hypothesis. No R=200 run has been done.

  Why it's defensible. The R=50 hypothesis is the standard explanation in the
  BLP literature (Nevo 2001). The qualitative comparison (shrinkage beats BLP on
   shock recovery) holds regardless of the sigma value because shock recovery is
   measured by MAE(ξ̂ − ξ*), not σ.

  Research risk. High if asked "why is your BLP sigma so wrong?" You cannot
  currently prove it is R=50 without running the R=200 comparison.

  How to talk about it. "The sigma bias is a finite-R artefact of the Monte
  Carlo share inversion. At R=50, the GMM objective surface is noisy enough to
  systematically select σ̂ below the truth. This is documented in the BLP
  literature (Nevo 2001). The shock recovery comparison — which is the paper's
  primary claim — is not affected by sigma estimation because we compute MAE(ξ̂ −
   ξ*) directly."

  CD-8 — The equivariance test does not cover authors_mode=True

  Evidence. test_equivariance.py:11: instantiates with default
  authors_mode=False. The AuthorsFeaturelessNetTF operates only on the
  availability indicator (no item_ids), so equivariance is determined by
  column-position encoding.

  Why it's defensible. The Bonus 1 Halo backbone uses authors_mode=False. The
  authors-mode net is not claimed to be equivariant in the same sense — it
  encodes brand identity by position, and equivariance holds only when items are
   permuted with their column positions, which is the correct interpretation for
   a fixed-universe model.

  Research risk. Low. The equivariance claim is made for the simplified path,
  which is the path actually used in Bonus 1.

  How to talk about it. "The simplified path is equivariant under simultaneous
  permutation of items and their IDs. The authors-mode network encodes brand
  identity by column position — equivariance holds under the same simultaneous
  permutation, but it is not tested separately."
